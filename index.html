🩺 Section 1

The Problem

In Kazakhstan, people in rural settlements rely on mid-level personnel, however there is evidence that workforce planning has mostly favored physicians. According to Ospanova et al. (2026), the ratio of nurse to physician in Kazakhstan saw a decline: for every physician there were 2.62 nurses in 2012 and that figure became 2.3 nurses in 2024, which is below the 3:1 ratio as recommended by WHO. Moreover, while the number of physicians in rural settlements grew since 2012, the density of mid-level staff levelled off. Despite the fact that the quantity of mid-level personnel rose by 67% between 2003 and 2024, this increase marginally exceeded the overall population growth (Ospanova et al., 2026).

As mentioned by the European Observatory on Health Systems and Policies & World Health Organization Regional Office for Europe (2022), rural areas experience a shortage in health workers even though there is a high number in general. From 2000 to 2020, maternal mortality saw a decrease by 76.8%, while neonatal mortality decreased by 79.2% from 2000 to 2022 (WHO, 2024, p.6). Since rural areas rely on mid-level personnel, we can infer that mid-level staff may be part of these outcomes, though the figures do not directly indicate their contribution. This suggests the need for balanced incentives for physicians and mid-level staff.

According to the review provided by the Office of the Premier Minister of Kazakhstan (2026), the support made for medical workers resulted in the drop of the shortage of doctors in rural areas by 13.8% and mid-level workers by only 1%. The review also mentions that 529 doctors working in shortage specialties received 8.5 million tenge as a one-time payment for signing contract for 5-year-service. The key word here is “doctors” not “mid-level workers”. This makes us suggest that the government’s most visible incentives target the shortage of doctors even though the government recognizes the shortage of mid-level personnel.

Overall, this raises a meaningful question: Why does the government’s planning keep prioritizing physicians even though the rural settlements rely on feldshers and nurses and how does this structural bias affect rural residents?

🔎 Section 2

Background & Data

Mapping the data landscape

The Bureau of National Statistics, ministries, international organisations provide data about broad-scale figures and averages. The BNS gives numbers for mid-level staff generally, European Observatory on Health Systems and Policies & World Health Organization Regional Office for Europe (2022) and WHO (2024) measure national health staff density, and the World Bank (2025) tracks national health expenditure and out-of-pocket costs. However, these sources measure aggregates broadly hiding disparities and the realities of locals.

The statistics suggest that official reports from the government often provide composite figures about mid-level workers without showing differences in the numbers of specific professionals like nurses, feldshers and midwives. For instance, The BNS report provides figures of mid-level personnel in general and only by regions without accounting for differences in them. Similarly, in his letter written in 2025, the Prime-Minister mentioned that while considering a request regarding the medical personnel in rural areas, the deficiency of middle medical workers was 835 in rural settlements (Prime Minister of the Republic of Kazakhstan, 2025). This combination makes the real data about the availability of types of medical cadres, which areas of Kazakhstan face issues of distribution, or how workload changes for remaining health professionals invisible. We cannot know how many positions are open where, as the statistics may have provided data combining far districts and villages.

The data given by WHO also shows this imbalance. According to the figure 5 in data from the European Observatory on Health Systems and Policies & World Health Organization Regional Office for Europe (2022), the proportion of physicians is larger in Kazakhstan than in the WHO European area overall (p.11). In contrast, Litvinova et al. (2025) mention that nurse numbers are below the average compared to the same region. Moreover, although the World Bank (2025) mentions that in 2023, 27.64% of current health expenditure was out-of-pocket expenditure, whether it accounts for travel costs of distant rural residents to district centers is an open question.

Who falls through the gaps?

Rural mid-level workers fall through the cracks of the system because official statistics provide data that aggregate them into “mid-level” staff. Because of this, information about their workload and geographical isolation remains invisible. Since the data do not account for the breakdown of mid-level personnel, the residents may not always receive adequate primary care. The government may also fall through the gap because due to the lack of data about the distribution of mid-level workers, their policies and financial incentives directed to widening rural access may be ineffective.

How far can the numbers be trusted?

I would say these data are largely reliable at an aggregate level but it is hard to say they are reliable for identifying the access of rural residents. The statistics of 835 open mid-level positions in rural areas given by the Prime Minister and absolute numbers delivered by the BNS that come from Ministry of Health data are credible measurements for overall conditions and shortage. However, numbers are unreliable when we implement them for rural settlements.The composite figures for mid-level health workers make it less effective for policies to help rural communities as they hide important details like rural-urban differences.

📚 Section 3

Current State of Research

Despite the acknowledgement of limitations existing in healthcare staff information systems including the aggregation of mid-level personnel data, the existing literature does not provide adequate and specific mechanisms for collecting data about workload of the workers in rural areas.

1. Workforce supply, distribution and retention

Nashwan et al. (2026) argue that even though global nursing and midwifery supply has increased, significant disparities in nurse supply within countries are overlooked in global assessments. Many low and middle-income nations face shortages in rural and peripheral primary care facilities. While they suggest improving health workforce information systems would help the government capture maldistribution and propose predictive analytics for dynamic workforce allocation, they do not provide a mechanism to derive information from rural health workers’ notes.

Likewise, Jobalayeva et al. (2024) study rural healthcare staff distribution in Kazakhstan and found that despite the efforts to increase the number of rural healthcare professionals, such as the incentive scheme in 2009, the overall growth in the number was less substantial in per capita. Therefore, the authors emphasized the need for more effective ways to retain healthcare workers in such settlements. They also used the data from the Ministry of Health that combined nursing personnel into one category so they couldn’t use separate information for types of nurses. Aktayeva (2026) also studies the structural challenges nurses face in Kazakhstan, suggesting top-down legislative changes and education platforms to solve these issues. However, she does not propose an approach to assess daily responsibilities and workload of rural mid-level staff. She also mentions that her survey may have not accounted for nurses in rural settlements due to the absence of Internet access. This shows the invisibility issue this project is aiming to tackle.

Overall, while these articles acknowledge limitations linked to staff distribution, retention and data, they do not provide a practical mechanism for collecting information about the real workload and duties of rural mid-level workforce.

2. Regional health infrastructure and digitalization

Regionally, the comparative study of Kyrgyzstan, Tajikistan, Uzbekistan and Kazakhstan by Rechel et al. (2023) shows distinctions in the mid-level workforce’s organization. Among these countries, Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan, and Tajikistan retain feldsher-midwifery positions, while Uzbekistan mostly has physician posts. Among recommendations, they propose more efforts in human resources planning and introducing information systems for collection and reporting of quality-care-data.

According to Haruna et al.’s (2025) narrative review, there are still issues like urban-rural disparities, high out-of-pocket costs, economic instability despite the efforts Kazakhstan has made for healthcare. Although they suggest digitalization as a solution to the issues, their recommendations are limited to mobile technologies in emergencies. They also utilize secondary data and do not account for the reflections of those who experience the issue.

Generally, these articles focus on broad health reforms and digitalization, but do not explain how daily frontline documentation can help workforce planning.

3. Geographical disparities

Shaltynov et al. (2022) evaluate the accessibility of primary healthcare in north-eastern Kazakhstani districts applying a two-step floating catchment area method in accordance with the number of physicians provided by the Ministry of Health. They used the Gini index and Lorenz curve to assess accessibility inequality. According to them, most rural areas located in this region turned out to have low availability of primary healthcare services. They found that 89% of the population in East Kazakhstan had sufficient access and those who had low access composed less than 15% of the population. One of the gaps of this research is that it studies only physicians instead of including other healthcare personnel like nurses and feldshers. It also relied on data from 2009, while I aim to use up-to-date information about rural areas.

Gap

As we can see, most of the research about Kazakhstani healthcare focuses on the quantity of health workers, geographical allocation, and governmental incentives. I could not find any study that could help with the invisibility of mid-level health workers in rural settlements, and combine mid-level workload. My project will fill the gap through a visibility layer that with the assistance of AI derives data from rural healthcare staff’s everyday notes and provides them to planners. This helps to resolve the aggregated staff data issue making the mid-level personnel’s work visible, possibly leading to effective planning policies.

🛰️ Section 4

The AI-assisted solution, and what it would cost

I would propose a “visibility layer” that functions with AI-assistance and is run by the Ministry of Health in Kazakhstan. To my knowledge, there is no comparable model. It has three components:

  1. Digitalization of records. Mid-level healthcare workers in rural settlements take pictures of their paper records with smartphones. An Optical Character Recognition model of the kind used in Meza recognizes handwritten words and structures them by positions of workers and locations, which helps to address the issue of a composite mid-level figure. A district health department staff member designated to access patient information checks the data that the model finds unclear.
  2. Analysis of records. The AI model identifies missed care and follow-ups that helps to summarize activity for every post.
  3. Estimation of workload. A district planner assesses the workload of feldsher and nurse posts in each rural settlement using the activity recorded and follow-up tasks that are still open in relation to the population served by posts. I should note, the third component follows from my proposal and has no documented evidence.

The first component is based on Meza, an AI tool introduced by the World Food Programme and Charitable Analytics International. It obtains health data related to nutrition from remote clinics, which helps them with timely and effective nutrition delivery (WFP, 2025). Its ability to work with limited Internet access helps workers transfer data faster and registered users can transfer them to different data analysis tools (Charitable Analytics, n.d.). In the first half of 2022, the model demonstrated high transcription accuracy in 94% of analyzed logbooks. In Rwanda, it provided more than 98% accurately transcribed data (WFP, 2025). This information is self-reported by WFP. Also, I did not find evidence that this works for Kazakh and Russian handwriting so its implementation for mid-level workers in Kazakhstani villages is only my inference and should be tested in the pilot.

We implement a non-profit organization Living Goods’ approach for the second and third parts. They help to improve community health workers’ potential by organizing support and training. Their case shows how frontline data can be transformed into planning information, even though they provided self-reported evidence. Their app provides the analysis of frontline data and identifies missed healthcare opportunities through Smart Supervision Assistant increasing household visitation from 72% to 92-94% according to the Living Goods’ self-report (Living Goods, 2026). I must note, the assistant is integrated with Kenya’s eCHIS and it works using digitized data. In my project it will be provided by the first component, in which Meza is a precedent. Also, CHWs are mostly volunteers who receive allowances, while mid-level workers are trained and receive salaries, but they both provide frontline healthcare. Although the AI-powered app is designed for different purposes like prioritizing follow-ups and identifying missed care opportunities, according to my inference, we can adapt it in Kazakhstan to measure frontline data and assess the workload of mid-level workers which is absent in the reports. Smart Supervision Assistant converts missed care opportunities into follow-up tasks. According to my own inference, by counting open follow-up tasks feldshers or nurses have to complete in rural settlements, we can estimate the workload of mid-level staff for every post.

🌱 Environment

According to Luccioni et al. (2024), my proposed model is considered task-specific and they will consume less energy compared to multi-task models. While their BLOOM model is not necessarily comparable to my project, its costs can still be used as upper contrast.

Assuming that there is 2.9 Wh per query (Luccioni et al., 2024, p.3) and I use it as an upper proxy for one photographed record daily for 4275 posts (Turgambayeva et al., 2021), the system would utilize 12.4 kWh daily, which is below 40.32 kWh daily used for BLOOM deployment (Luccioni et al., 2024, p.2). If posts photograph several pages, it may increase substantially. Kazakhstan’s carbon intensity of electricity in 2025 was 813 g CO2/kWh (Global Grid, 2026). Therefore, hosting on Kazakhstan’s carbon-intensive grid would produce nearly 3.7 tonnes of CO2 a year and at 0.18-1.1 litres of water per kWh (Luccioni et al., 2024, p.5), nearly 815-4980 litres a year, excluding manufacturing and idle consumption. Since we host the system on our national grid, our carbon footprint will arise. Kazakhstani legislation doesn’t have defined environmental requirements (Buribayeva et al., 2026). This has legal costs, as according to the article 8 of the law on AI (2025), the creation and functioning of AI should consider energy efficiency.

💰 Economics

According to the Law on Artificial Intelligence in Kazakhstan, the government is responsible for implementing AI policy in priority sectors (Article 13.1 (7), 28.2, Law on AI, 2025). As it doesn’t list priority sectors, it is hard to confirm whether rural healthcare workforce would be considered a priority. If it is not classified as a priority sector, the Ministry of Health might have to lobby. Based on this, it can be assumed that the governmental budget will be used to pay for development and hosting.

Smartphones for posts that do not have one, OCR hosting, a district health department staff member’s hours to check entries and training may be main costs. It is fair to note that we cannot provide cost-benefit figures precisely, as for that we would need real vendor quotes and data about how many posts actually have smartphones.

Those who profit would be vendors who receive income from equipment contracts and contracts for adapting an OCR model. Planners will benefit from the data provided.

🏛️ Governance and politics

As the Ministry of Health will have access to the data, their leakage and misuse is also possible, leading to the centralization of information. As mentioned by Sastry et al. (2024), visibility mechanisms might have 2 risks: 1) there might be a threat to personal privacy because of data leakage 2) as the Ministry will have sensitive information, suggesting that this could be misused for penalization of specific posts.

The workload data could also be repurposed for planning about ranking and salaries of mid-level workers, with authorities possibly closing the posts that have low activities, which does not align with the principles for responsible stewardship of AI provided by OECD (2024), AI actors needing to combat risks of the use beyond the intended purpose (Art 1.2 (b).

The system will be hosted inside Kazakhstan and the Ministry of Health of Kazakhstan will own the system and run it. Meza will be a precedent for the technique. There might be contracts with vendors for building the OCR component. However, they will only work with depersonalized samples. Only the ministry being a regulator and an operator may lead to a weak external oversight reminding of Buribayeva et al.’s (2026) risks of institutional concentration. Under the law on AI (2025), the layer is classified as medium-risk, closed and low autonomy (Art.17) . As the article 7 (2) mentions, staff will be informed about the procedure and consequences of the usage of AI. The article 17 (3) (3) prohibits AI that evaluates and classifies individuals for certain time based on social behavior or known or assumed personal characteristics with an exception for situations provided by law. It is an open question for the proposed solution because the system will evaluate posts not necessarily individuals and whether work performance is considered the same category is subject to interpretation.

🧑‍⚕️ Labor

The visibility layer mostly affects the rural mid-level staff. They will still fill their paper records and photograph them, which adds clerical work. Gmyrek et al. (2025) put clerical work in the highest exposure gradient 4 for Generative AI automation, but it is not about mid-level-staff, rather it is about clerical workers. Nevertheless, this type of clerical work may steal their time for their actual responsibility, which is clinical care and it can be comparable to Gray&Suri’s ghost work mentioned by Williams&Miceli (2023).

The Ministry of Health, as the system owner, must hold constant risk management to combat falsification in accordance with the Article 18 of the Law on AI (2025). As the EU AI Act mentions, AI systems monitoring and evaluating workers are high-risk (European Parliament & Council of the European Union 2024, Annex III, 4(b)), therefore our layer will measure posts not workers. If results are implemented for staff evaluation, according to Article 7 (2), affected individuals can use the right to be informed and to object automated decisions. Users also have the right to refuse to interact with AI if they are not obliged by law (Art. 16(1)). This suggests, if mid-level workers see some surveillance in the system they may refuse the system.

🔐 Data ethics

The system needs data from patients for missed care and settlement data for mid-level workforce information. Since the photographs contain patients’ names, the data will be depersonalized and pictures deleted before reaching planners. This is in accordance with the Article 61 of the Health Code as mentioned in the Ministry of Health Order, which writes that for analytical and statistical activity and research, personal data are depersonalised (Ministry of Health of the Republic of Kazakhstan, 2021).

Mid-level workers’ extra work may lead to falsification. As Okech et al. (2025) have mentioned, in a setting that pays for performance, high performance pressure means ethical issues such as data falsification, inaccuracies and burnout, sometimes leading to conscious data falsification in pursuit of targets. According to the Law on AI, article 27, assuming that post records are a data library, the Ministry must provide data quality and audits, as not doing so may lead to inaccuracies in planning (Art. 20 (2)).

Most importantly, the pilot to be conducted should assess whether an OCR output of Kazakh and Russian notes matches the results of manual review in one region before scaling. Its success should be measured with an agreed minimum accuracy level. Also, it should test 2 and 3 components as there is no evidence for workload estimates. If the Ministry of health of Kazakhstan can prevent the misuse of data, lobby for priority sector funding, hosts the system in a low-carbon condition or utilizes renewable energy, compensates labor of mid-level workers for recording and photographing and district reviewers, we can implement visibility layer for the collection of planning information and assessing the workload of mid-level workers, which will be used to provide adequate primary care to rural residents.
